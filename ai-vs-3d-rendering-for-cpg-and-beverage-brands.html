AI vs 3D rendering (CGI) for CPG and beverage brands: an honest comparison

Use 3D rendering (CGI) when you need the exact pack: every angle, every flavor swap, and glass or liquid that has to look right, all from one model built to your real dimensions and label files. Use AI image generation when the pack already exists and you need lots of scenes around it, then check every image against the real product, because the model redraws the label and the shape each time. You can also use both: a 3D render or a real packshot as the reference, and AI for the scenes around it.

By Abhi Chawla, founder · Last updated: 2026-11-13

100 Creatives sells AI-made product imagery and ads. We don't build 3D models, so this page says when to hire a 3D studio or a photographer instead. The brand images on this page are spec concepts, not client work.

3D and CGI

What 3D rendering does for a can, bottle or pouch

A 3D artist builds a digital model of your pack from its dimensions, the dieline or a physical sample. They wrap your actual label artwork around it and give each surface a material: aluminum, clear or tinted glass, foil, matte paper, the liquid inside. Then they light a virtual set and render. Once the model exists, a new angle, a new backdrop or a new flavor label is another render on the same model, with no rebuild.

That build-once model is why 3D suits beverage brands with pack families. Outshinery, a studio that makes product imagery for wine, beer, spirits, RTD, cider and cannabis brands, makes product shots from your label file and requires an initial product shot before it makes any other imagery. After that, an updated product shot starts at 0 credits per image, with vintage, alcohol content and liquid color changes listed as free (Outshinery pricing, checked November 2026).

3D is strongest in four places:

  • Label text. The label is your print file, so the ingredients, the legal copy and the logo are the real words.
  • Pack geometry. The can's proportions, the bottle's shoulder and the cap come from measurements, so every angle matches the real pack.
  • Glass, liquid and condensation. The artist sets these up on the model, so the liquid color and the droplets stay the same from frame to frame.
  • Packs that don't exist yet. A new flavor can be rendered from the artwork before the first production run.

The cost sits up front. Someone has to build the model, and a convincing glass bottle takes a skilled modeller. Scenes with people, food and props add work on top of the packshot, which is why Outshinery prices a lifestyle image from 2 credits against 1 for a product shot. If you build in-house, the software is the smaller decision. Blender is released under the GNU GPL, which gives you the right to use it for any purpose, and the license covers the application, not the artwork you make with it (Blender manual, checked November 2026). KeyShot Studio Professional is $1,299 per user per year, billed annually, with a 14-day free trial (KeyShot pricing, checked November 2026). You still need someone who can model and light a pack well.

Clothing raises a different problem, fabric drape on a body rather than glass and liquid. Our AI vs 3D rendering comparison for apparel covers that case.

AI image generation

What AI image generation does well, and where it drifts

An AI image model takes a reference photo of your pack and a written brief, then draws a new picture: the can on a picnic table, the bottle in an ice bucket, the pouch in a gym bag. A new scene is a new prompt rather than a new build, so AI is the fastest of the three methods at scene and lifestyle variety.

The model has never measured your can, though, and it redraws the product on every render. Three things drift:

  • Label text. Small print turns into near-words, letters swap, and the barcode and nutrition panel become texture. Big logos hold up better than small print, but they can still shift.
  • Pack geometry. A slim can turns standard, a bottle's shoulder changes, and the cap or ring-pull changes shape between frames of the same set.
  • Color and liquid. The label color moves with the scene light, and the liquid's color, fill line and bubbles change from one frame to the next. Our guide to color accuracy in AI product photography covers how to check it.

Expect to throw most renders away. Before Overs existed (it's our founder's AI photo tool, and we make our images with it today), we built four campaigns in 2026 by hand in Figma Weave with AI image models. Across them, 361 renders produced 45 keepers, about 8 renders per usable photo. The one closest to this page, a set of brownie food ads with motion, took 120 renders to get 10 keepers.

Photo shoot

Where a real photo shoot still wins

A camera records the product that exists, so the label, the shape and the color start out right, within the limits of the lighting and the retouching. Book a shoot when you need the real thing on record: a real pour with real foam, condensation on the actual can, real people drinking it, or a demonstration a shopper will compare with what arrives at the door.

The cost is logistics. You need product, a set, a stylist and a photographer, and a label change means a reshoot or retouching. Shoot prices vary by photographer, so get a quote. A shoot also gives you clean reference photos for AI scenes later. Our guide to when you still need a real photo shoot goes through the cases where a camera wins.

The hybrid

The hybrid: an exact packshot as the reference, AI for the scenes

The pack is the part shoppers check and the part AI redraws. So lock the pack first, then generate around it:

  1. Get one true packshot per SKU. A 3D render from the final artwork, or a real photo under even light. Every later image gets checked against it.
  2. Use it as the reference for AI scenes. Kitchen counters, coolers, seasonal sets, ad layouts.
  3. Put the true pack back where the label must be read. On close-ups and product page images, composite the real packshot into the scene instead of keeping the AI's redrawn pack.
  4. Check the finished set against the physical pack, not against other renders.

A flavor launch might be one 3D model, or one afternoon of packshots, followed by lifestyle scenes and ad versions that all reuse it.

Before you approve

What to check before you approve 3D renders or AI images

For 3D renders

  • The model was built from the final dieline or a production pack, not an early mockup.
  • The label is the latest print file. Check the version, the legal copy and the barcode.
  • The materials match the real finish: matte or gloss, foil, embossing, cap color, can tab.
  • The liquid color and the fill line match a real pour of the product.
  • You know who owns the model file, in what format, and what a label change costs.

For AI images

  • Read every word on the label at full size. Small print, the nutrition panel and the barcode fail first.
  • Put the image next to the real pack and compare proportions: height against width, the shoulder, the cap, the can tab.
  • Check color against the production pack under daylight-balanced light, not against another render.
  • Lay the set out in a grid. The fill line, bubbles, condensation and label position should match from frame to frame.
  • Check the hands, the glassware and anything else the product touches.

When something is off, name the SKU, the spot and the direction in your note, such as “the can on the cooler is too tall, match the reference.” Vague notes get you another round of guesses.

Frequently asked
questions

Is AI or 3D rendering better for beverage product images?

It depends on the asset. 3D rendering is better for exact packshots, glass and liquid that must repeat, turntables, and label swaps across a flavor range, because every image comes from one model built to your real pack. AI image generation is better for lots of lifestyle scenes and ad variations around a pack that already exists. You can also use a 3D render or a real packshot as the reference and AI for the scenes around it.

How much does 3D product rendering cost for a bottle or can?

Outshinery, which renders wine, beer, spirits, RTD and cider bottles from label files, lists $129 per credit bought singly, with a product shot from 1 credit and a lifestyle image from 2, plus a $29 per image Lite service for standard bottles with flat-printed labels (checked November 2026). Many CGI studios quote per project instead. To build in-house, KeyShot Studio Professional is $1,299 per user per year, and Blender is released under the GNU GPL.

Can AI image generators get my label text right?

Not reliably. The model redraws the label on every render, so small print, ingredient lists, nutrition panels and barcodes often come out as near-words or texture, and logos can shift. Read every word at full size before you approve an image. For close-ups and product page images where shoppers read the label, place a true packshot from a real photo or a 3D render into the scene instead of keeping the AI version.

Do I need a 3D model to use AI for my product images?

No. Clean photos of the production pack, taken from the front and sides under even light, work as references. A 3D render helps when the pack isn't printed yet or when you want one exact reference for every flavor. Either way, every AI image gets checked against that reference, so it has to show the final label and the real shape of the pack.

When should a CPG brand hire a 3D studio instead of using AI?

Hire a 3D studio when the pack doesn't physically exist yet, when you need exact-geometry turntables or 360 spins, when a pack family has many flavor or label swaps, or when glass, liquid and condensation must look the same across a whole set. In each case one accurate model gives you the same pack in every frame. Before you sign, ask who owns the model file and what a label change costs.
