What Meta restricts in health ads

Meta's Health and Wellness ad standard, last revised July 22, 2026 according to its change log, allows supplement and wellness ads but restricts what they claim and who sees them (Meta Transparency Center, checked October 2026). Ads for dietary, health, weight loss or weight gain products, including supplements, must target people 18 or older. General food products, protein products, general-wellbeing products such as fitness equipment and health clubs, non-permanent cosmetics such as creams and makeup, and dental products such as teeth whitening are exempt from that age rule.

The content rules are short and specific. An ad can't make a claim, even one attributed to a doctor or health organization, to cure, heal or eliminate any disease on Meta's list: diabetes, herpes, thyroid disease, psoriasis, Ebola, cancer, autism, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, ALS and HIV. Claims about managing symptoms of those conditions fall outside that ban. Weight loss ads can't show a close-up of someone pinching fat, and no ad in the category can attack a person's appearance or body. Weight loss ads also can't claim a wearable product delivers results on its own, and skin whitening or bleaching products that permanently change skin color are barred. Meta also bars clickbait, meaning sensational language or a promise of a specific result within a set timeframe without a qualifier.

Some things people assume are banned are allowed for 18+ audiences. A supplement ad can show someone using the product and the effect, as long as it states how long noticeable results took, and cosmetic products can show a before-and-after. The same colostrum, magnesium or probiotic product can run one ad that clears review and another that doesn't, based on wording alone.

What the FTC expects on top of Meta's review

Clearing Meta's ad review is not the same as clearing US advertising law. The Federal Trade Commission's Health Products Compliance Guidance replaced the agency's 1998 dietary supplement advertising guide and covers foods, supplements, over-the-counter drugs, health equipment, diagnostic tests and health apps (FTC.gov, checked September 2026). Advertising must be truthful, and a claim about a health benefit needs competent and reliable scientific evidence behind it before the ad runs. Customer testimonials don't count as that evidence.

This is why a health and wellness ad creative brief needs an answer to "what evidence backs this claim" before a designer opens a layout. Meta's review checks the ad. The FTC's standard applies to the brand regardless of what any platform approves.

TikTok advertising policies for health and wellness products

Most wellness brands start on Meta, so most guides stop there. TikTok spreads its health rules across three policy pages, and a batch built only to Meta's standard can fail on any of them. The North America handbook this page cited until October 2026 now redirects to TikTok's policy index, so the links below go to the individual policies.

Weight loss and muscle gain

TikTok's Weight Management and Body Image policy, last updated September 2026, lists weight loss supplements and meal replacement products as products that "may be allowed" in the United States and Canada, subject to age restrictions, local licensing and the rest of TikTok's policies (TikTok Business Help Center, Weight Management and Body Image, checked October 2026). Weight loss surgery can run too, but only after a TikTok sales representative confirms eligibility. Any weight loss or muscle gain claim must target people 18 or older and must be made through the promotion of a healthy lifestyle. The policy names four claims it won't accept: unrealistic weight loss or muscle gain, encouragement of an unhealthy relationship with food, a product that works without diet or exercise, and results that are easy or guaranteed.

That reverses what this page said on October 17, when TikTok's North America handbook prohibited weight loss supplements outright. A weight-management brand that kept a SKU off TikTok on that basis can reopen the question, with the creative built around the routine the product sits in rather than the pill.

Body image

The same policy bars ads and landing pages that shame people about their bodies, suggest there is an ideal body type, or claim that looking a certain way makes someone happier, more popular, more desirable or more confident. The clause reaches past weight loss products: a skincare, supplement or fitness caption that ties a slimmer waist to confidence breaks it even when the product claim is clean. Meta's Health and Wellness standard has a narrower version: no ad in the category can attack a person's appearance or body.

Supplements, OTC medicines and CBD

TikTok's Healthcare and Pharmaceuticals policy, also updated September 2026, sets rules market by market (TikTok Business Help Center, Healthcare and Pharmaceuticals, checked October 2026). In the United States, over-the-counter medicines and medical devices may run to audiences 18 and older, prescription medicine needs FDA approval and 18+ targeting, and online pharmacies need certification from a body such as NABP or LegitScript. CBD gets the narrowest lane: topical hemp products under 0.3% THC only, LegitScript certification, a TikTok sales representative and an audience aged 25 or older. All other CBD products are not allowed, so a CBD gummy brand can't buy TikTok reach for that SKU.

Claims and before-and-afters

TikTok's Misleading and False Content policy, last updated April 2026, bars ads that promise or exaggerate a product's effect and absolute terms about time, region or brand (TikTok Business Help Center, Misleading and False Content, checked October 2026). TikTok's own examples include "Get slim legs right away", claims of cures for incurable diseases, a before-and-after showing wrinkles disappearing under a cream, and "Number 1 essence on TikTok". Meta lets cosmetic products show a before-and-after to 18+ audiences, so a skincare or collagen brand usually needs one cut for Meta and an ingredient-led cut for TikTok. Our skincare ad creatives guide covers what a fair before-and-after needs.

Our working rule for TikTok health creative: state the ingredient, the routine and the time to results, show the person's day rather than their body, and keep anything that reads as a guarantee out of the hook. If a creator will say the claim on camera, the same rules apply to their script; UGC ad creatives covers briefing them.

Health and wellness advertising on Google

Google restricts by ingredient and by targeting. Its Healthcare and medicines policy bars unapproved substances regardless of claims of legality, including supplements with active pharmaceutical or dangerous ingredients, products containing ephedra, and products that imply they work as well as prescription drugs (Google Ads policy, Healthcare and medicines, checked October 2026). Its Health in personalized advertising policy treats health as a sensitive interest category: ads about physical or mental health conditions, including chronic ones, can't use Customer Match, your data segments, audience expansion or lookalike segments, and nobody under 18 gets personalized ads (Google Ads policy, Health in personalized advertising, checked October 2026). The policy covers condition-related content, so whether a sleep or stress product falls under it depends on how the ad and landing page frame it. Settle that with your media buyer before the brief, because the framing decides both the copy and the audience.

What compliant creative looks like

In practice, the ads that clear review and hold up legally share a few habits.

Structure-function language over outcome promises

"Supports gut health" describes a mechanism. "Cures your IBS" is a disease claim Meta and the FTC both restrict. The gap between those two sentences is where most rejected health ads live, and it is closable with a copy pass rather than a redesign.

Ingredient and mechanism storytelling instead of transformation shots

Close-up pinches, scale numbers and side-by-side weight-loss photos are exactly what Meta's policy targets. Ad creative built around the ingredient, the ritual of using the product, or the specific mechanism it claims to support sidesteps that category of rejection entirely, and it tends to photograph better than a stock transformation shot.

Social proof framed as individual results

A customer quote can run, but the FTC guidance says a testimonial reporting results more dramatic than buyers can generally expect is likely deceptive, and a "results not typical" line doesn't fix it. The ad needs a clear disclosure of what a typical customer can expect, or a quote whose result already sits in that range. Picking the quote is a claims decision, so it belongs in the brief, before design.
