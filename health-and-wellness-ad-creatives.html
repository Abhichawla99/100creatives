What Meta restricts in health ads

Meta's Health and Wellness ad standard, last revised July 22, 2026 according to its change log, allows supplement and wellness ads but restricts what they claim and who sees them (Meta Transparency Center, checked September 2026). Ads for dietary, health, weight loss or weight gain products, including supplements, must target people 18 or older. General food products, protein products and general-wellbeing services such as gyms are exempt from that age rule.

The content rules are short and specific. An ad can't make a claim, even one attributed to a doctor or health organization, to cure, heal or eliminate any disease on Meta's list: diabetes, herpes, thyroid disease, psoriasis, Ebola, cancer, autism, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, ALS and HIV. Claims about managing symptoms of those conditions fall outside that ban. Weight loss ads can't show a close-up of someone pinching fat, and no ad in the category can attack a person's appearance or body. Meta also bars clickbait, meaning sensational language or a promise of a specific result within a set timeframe without a qualifier.

Some things people assume are banned are allowed for 18+ audiences. A supplement ad can show someone using the product and the effect, as long as it states how long noticeable results took, and cosmetic products can show a before-and-after. The same colostrum, magnesium or probiotic product can run one ad that clears review and another that doesn't, based on wording alone.

What the FTC expects on top of Meta's review

Clearing Meta's ad review is not the same as clearing US advertising law. The Federal Trade Commission's Health Products Compliance Guidance replaced the agency's 1998 dietary supplement advertising guide and covers foods, supplements, over-the-counter drugs, health equipment, diagnostic tests and health apps (FTC.gov, checked September 2026). Advertising must be truthful, and a claim about a health benefit needs competent and reliable scientific evidence behind it before the ad runs. Customer testimonials don't count as that evidence.

This is why a health and wellness ad creative brief needs an answer to "what evidence backs this claim" before a designer opens a layout. Meta's review checks the ad. The FTC's standard applies to the brand regardless of what any platform approves.

What compliant creative looks like

In practice, the ads that clear review and hold up legally share a few habits.

Structure-function language over outcome promises

"Supports gut health" describes a mechanism. "Cures your IBS" is a disease claim Meta and the FTC both restrict. The gap between those two sentences is where most rejected health ads live, and it is closable with a copy pass rather than a redesign.

Ingredient and mechanism storytelling instead of transformation shots

Close-up pinches, scale numbers and side-by-side weight-loss photos are exactly what Meta's policy targets. Ad creative built around the ingredient, the ritual of using the product, or the specific mechanism it claims to support sidesteps that category of rejection entirely, and it tends to photograph better than a stock transformation shot.

Social proof framed as individual results

A customer quote can run, but the FTC guidance says a testimonial reporting results more dramatic than buyers can generally expect is likely deceptive, and a "results not typical" line doesn't fix it. The ad needs a clear disclosure of what a typical customer can expect, or a quote whose result already sits in that range. Picking the quote is a claims decision, so it belongs in the brief, before design.
