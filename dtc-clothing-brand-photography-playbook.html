DTC clothing brand photography: the shot list per style

By Abhi Chawla, founder · Last updated: 2026-10-01

A DTC clothing brand needs three sets of images for every style it sells: product page shots that answer fit, fabric and color, ad images cropped for Meta and TikTok placements, and a smaller set for email and the product catalog that feeds retargeting. Plan all three as one shot list per style and make them together, so the olive jacket is the same olive on the product page, in the ad and in the abandoned-cart email. This playbook gives that shot list, the specs that bound it, and a way to schedule it around a drop.

Why a DTC clothing brand plans photos per style, not per channel

Most DTC clothing brands grow their photography one channel at a time. The product page shoot happens first, because the store can't launch without it. Ads come later, cropped out of the product page set or made by a different freelancer. Email gets whatever is left. Six months in, the same jacket appears in three slightly different colors and on three different bodies, and the shopper who clicked an ad lands on a product page that doesn't look like what she clicked.

That mismatch costs money after the sale. The National Retail Federation and Happy Returns put US retail returns for 2024 at $890 billion, 16.9% of annual sales, and found that 51% of Gen Z shoppers bracket, ordering several sizes or colors with a plan to send most back (NRF, December 2024, checked September 2026). Clothing is exposed to it because fit and color are exactly what a screen hides. Every image a shopper sees before buying is part of the size guide, whether you planned it that way or not.

The fix is a single shot list per style, written before anything is made, that covers every place the style will appear. A DTC brand is the one kind of clothing business that controls all of those places: its own store, its own ads, its own email list and its own catalog feed. That control is the advantage, and planning per channel throws it away.

What each image has to answer

A shopper asks three things about clothing on a screen: will it fit me, what does the fabric feel like, and is that the real color. Each line in the shot list answers one of them.

Fit lives in the on-model front, back and side shots. State the model's height and the size worn on the product page, and repeat it in the fit callout ad. A shopper between sizes reads that line before she reads the size chart. If your range runs from XS to 3X, show more than one body, because a single model in a size small answers the fit question for only some of your customers.

Fabric lives in the close-up: the knit, the brushed back of a fleece, a waistband pulled between two hands. It stands in for touching the garment in a store, and it is the shot most often skipped when a team is short on time.

Color lives everywhere, and it drifts everywhere. Warm lifestyle light turns cream to yellow, and every export and compression step shifts it again. Check every image against the physical sample or the swatch under neutral light, and treat the product page front shot as the reference the ads must match. Planning photos around a drop

DTC clothing runs on drops: a new colorway, a capsule, a restock, a seasonal collection. Each one has a date the email goes out and a list of styles that need images by then. Work back from that date.

Three to four weeks before launch

Lock the style list, the colorways and the hero styles you will run ads behind. Collect the inputs: sample photos or 3D files of each style, your size guide, fit model measurements, and any fit or fabric claims you already make. For a brand new to us, this is also when the brand intake happens: visual language, model preferences, colorways and export specs.

Two weeks before

Product page sets for every style, starting with the hero styles. Review color against samples and fit against the size guide before anything else gets made, because a wrong color on the product page spreads into every ad cropped from it.

One week before

Ad variations for the hero styles, the launch email hero and the catalog images. This is where a 48-hour turnaround on batches and on revisions earns its keep: a buyer change to a colorway or a late restock date still fits before the send.

Launch and the two weeks after

Read the first spend. Scale the ad formats that work across more colorways, and replace the ones that don't. Restock and back-in-stock emails reuse the product page set, which is why it has to be right first.

Where AI imagery fits for clothing, and where it doesn't

100 Creatives makes product and campaign images without a physical shoot, using Overs, an AI image tool built for brand-matched product photos. Overs is made by Abhi Chawla, founder of 100 Creatives, and both are brands of 2587689 Alberta Ltd., so weigh our view of it with that in mind.

Clothing is one of the harder categories for generated images, and our own records show why. Before Overs existed, we built four campaigns in 2026 by hand with AI image models. Men's trousers on a model took 40 renders to get 11 usable images. Surf apparel on one model took 163 renders to get 12. The rejects were mostly fit and fabric errors: a hem at the wrong length, a waistband that changed between frames, a print that redrew itself. A playbook built on generated images needs a review step that checks every image against the sample, and on-model photography at scale covers how that review works across a large catalog.

Some clothing still needs a camera. Handwork and embellishment a customer will inspect up close, a founder or a named model wearing the collection, try-on content, and fabrics whose drape changes with every step are all cases where a real shoot is the honest choice. When you need a real photo shoot walks through those cases. A mixed plan is common: shoot the hero styles once, then make the colorway and ad variations from that reference.

Frequently asked questions

What photos does a DTC clothing brand need for each style?
A product page set (front, back and side on a model, a fabric close-up and one flat or ghost-mannequin shot, with front and back repeated per colorway), a clean catalog image per colorway for Meta catalog ads, and, for hero styles only, ad variations such as a fit callout, a fabric close-up and a colorway grid. Add a wide email hero and a collection banner for the launch.
How many product images does a clothing drop need?
Count it from the shot list. A style with four colorways and five product page shots per colorway needs 20 images before any ad. Thirty styles at that rate is 600 product images, plus ad variations for the eight or ten hero styles you plan to spend behind. Writing the number down early shows whether a studio day, a virtual shoot or a mix fits the launch date.
What image size should clothing product photos be on Shopify?
Shopify says square product images usually display best at 2048 x 2048 pixels and allows up to 5000 x 5000 pixels or 25 megapixels, with files under 20 MB. Use one consistent aspect ratio for featured images so collection pages line up (Shopify Help Center, checked October 2026). Meta catalog images need to be at least 500 x 500 pixels.
Can a DTC clothing brand use AI images instead of a photo shoot?
For many styles, yes, as long as every image is checked against the physical sample for fit, length and color. Clothing is a hard category for generated images: in our 2026 records, trousers on a model took 40 renders for 11 usable images. Handwork, try-on content and a named model or founder still call for a real shoot, and many brands shoot hero styles and generate the variations.
When should a clothing brand plan photography for a drop?
Three to four weeks before the launch email: lock styles, colorways and hero styles, and collect samples, the size guide and fit model measurements. Make product page sets two weeks out, then ads, email and catalog images in the final week. A 48-hour turnaround on batches and revisions leaves room for late colorway changes before the send.
