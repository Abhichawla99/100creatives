Why skincare ads are harder than makeup ads

A lipstick ad can prove itself in one frame: here is the color, on skin. A serum can't. Most skincare results take weeks, happen gradually and look small in a photo, so the ad is tempted to fill the gap with words ("repairs", "renews", "clinically proven") or with an image of skin that no product produces. Both are where skincare ads get rejected by Meta, pulled by a regulator or ignored by a buyer who has seen the same poreless face a thousand times.

What a skincare ad can show without overclaiming is narrower and more useful: the texture of the formula, how it sits on skin right after application, the routine it belongs to, the ingredient the buyer already searched for, and a result from your own study when you have one. The makeup side of the category, where shade accuracy is the main job, is on beauty ad creatives. This page is about the part of the category where the words carry the risk.

Cosmetic claims and drug claims, word by word

In the United States, a cosmetic is meant for "cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering the appearance". A product intended to treat or prevent disease, or to affect the structure or function of the body, is a drug. The FDA's page on cosmetics and drugs (checked September 2026) says intended use can be shown by claims on the label, in advertising and on the internet, by what consumers expect, and by ingredients with a well-known therapeutic use. It names claims that make a product a drug, including increasing or decreasing melanin production and regenerating cells, and it says "cosmeceutical" has no meaning under the law.

Canada draws the same line with worked examples. Advertising Standards Canada's guidelines on non-therapeutic claims, written with Health Canada (October 2016 edition, checked September 2026), list what a cosmetic can and can't say. A few rows from that list show the pattern:

Cosmetic (acceptable)Therapeutic (needs drug authorization)
Smoothes wrinkles; wrinkles appear reducedRemoves wrinkles
Improves the look of acne scarsRemoves or reduces scars
Reduces the look or the signs of agingAny reference to action at a cellular level
Repairs dry skinTreats acne; heals (unqualified)

The difference is usually two words: "the look of". A hook writer chasing a stronger line drifts from the left column to the right one, and AI copy tools drift the same way because the right column sounds more confident. Our ads use the claims on your label and your approved claims list, and we don't add new ones. When a brief asks for a line from the right column, we flag it before we design around it.

Sunscreen and acne products are a different category

In the US, sunscreens are regulated as drugs, and a moisturizer or foundation with SPF is both a cosmetic and a drug. The FDA's consumer page on sunscreen (checked September 2026) says there is no such thing as a waterproof sunscreen, and that a product labeled water resistant must say whether it stays effective for 40 or 80 minutes when swimming or sweating. So an SPF ad shows the claim on the label, word for word: "water resistant (80 minutes)", never "waterproof", and the SPF number printed on the pack. Acne treatments sit in the same place in Canada, where "treats acne" is on the therapeutic side of the table above. If your product is an OTC drug, your regulatory advisor approves the claims first and we build the ads around them.

Numbers in skincare ads need a study behind them

"87% said skin felt smoother after 4 weeks" is the kind of line a skincare buyer stops for, and the kind a regulator reads closely. The UK Advertising Standards Authority's guidance on creams (updated January 2025, checked September 2026) says a claim like "80% of women said it reduced the appearance of their wrinkles" needs a consumer use or perception test behind it, and that claims of a physiological or cumulative effect need a much higher level of evidence. The same guidance says "anti-ageing" can be acceptable where the context makes clear the effect is temporary or applies to how wrinkles look.

That gives three practical rules for skincare statics:

  • A percentage goes in the ad only if it comes from your own study, in the words the study measured. "Felt smoother" and "is smoother" are different claims.
  • The timeframe goes next to the number. "After 4 weeks" is part of the claim, and dropping it for space changes what the ad says.
  • Perception results stay perception results. A self-assessment panel supports "said", "felt" and "noticed"; it doesn't support "clinically proven".

Where you don't have a study, the ad sells the texture, the routine or the ingredient instead. Those formats are below, and they don't need a number to work.

Real skin, not rendered skin

The ASA's guidance on production techniques in cosmetics ads (updated August 2025, checked September 2026) treats before-and-after images as objective claims, the same as written ones: the after has to be representative of what consumers can generally get, and the styling and photography for the after can't differ from the before. It also warns that a superimposed disclaimer doesn't excuse an image that would otherwise be misleading. Its own example of the line being crossed is removing or reducing lines and wrinkles around the eyes in an eye cream ad.

AI image tools cross that line by default. The models learned from millions of retouched beauty photos, so they produce skin with no pores, no texture, no redness and no fine lines. In a food ad that's scenery. In a skincare ad it's a promise the product can't keep, sitting exactly where the product is supposed to work. We explain why generated images drift toward the same polished look in why AI ads look the same. Our review step checks every skincare image for visible pores, natural texture and realistic skin before delivery, and how to review AI creative from an agency sets out the checklist you can hold us or anyone else to.

What Meta allows for skincare ads

Meta's Health and Wellness ad policy (Meta Transparency Center, checked September 2026) says ads promoting cosmetic products must be targeted to people at least 18 years old. Ads for cosmetic products can't contain statements of inferiority about physical appearance, meaning terms, descriptions or questions that attack someone's appearance, specific body parts or hygiene. The policy also bars ads for skin whitening or bleaching products that cause permanent skin color change, and claims to cure, heal or eliminate a listed set of incurable conditions, which includes psoriasis.

For a skincare brand that rules out three common hooks. "Embarrassed by your breakouts?" attacks appearance. A brightening serum can't be sold on permanent lightening. An eczema or psoriasis balm can talk about soothing the look and feel of dry, itchy skin, but not about clearing the condition. It also means a brand built on teen acne has to reach under-18s through organic content, because the Meta ads can't. The hook that survives all three rules is the result or the ritual: "Barrier cream for the second week of a ski trip", "The step between cleanser and SPF". Six skincare formats that work as statics

These are the formats we start a skincare brief with. None of them needs a claim your label doesn't already make.

1. The texture swipe

The formula smeared across a clean surface or the back of a hand, shot tight enough to see whether it's a gel, a milk, a balm or an oil. Texture is the first thing a buyer wants to know and the one thing a product page can't answer, so this format often carries a new product.

2. The active callout

The bottle plus the ingredient the buyer searched for, at the concentration printed on your label: "10% niacinamide", "0.3% retinal". The number has to match the pack exactly. Skincare buyers read ingredient lists, and a mismatch between the ad and the INCI list is the kind of thing that ends up in comments.

3. The routine slot

Where the product goes: after cleanser, before SPF; morning only; twice a week. It answers "will this fit with what I already use?", which is the question that stalls a first purchase of an active.

4. The study result

One number from your own study, with its timeframe and its base, and the product large enough to recognize. Only when the study supports the exact wording, per the section above.

5. The before and after

Allowed on the ASA's terms: same lighting, same styling, the same retouching on both, and an after a customer can get. For most skincare this means real photography from your study or from customers who agreed to it. We don't generate a before-and-after for a skincare result, because a generated after isn't evidence of anything.

6. The SPF in context

The sunscreen on a beach bag, in a gym locker, next to a ski pass, with the label claim in the copy exactly as printed. It sells the habit instead of the protection number, which keeps the ad clear of drug-claim wording beyond what the label says.

Which of these wins for your range is a testing question. How many ad creatives you need covers how many variations to run at a given budget, and health and wellness ad creatives covers ingestible beauty and supplement-style claims, which follow different rules.

Frequently asked questions

What can a skincare ad claim without becoming a drug ad?
Claims about how skin looks and feels: hydrates, smooths, reduces the look of fine lines, evens the look of skin tone. In the US, claims to treat or prevent disease or to change how the body works, such as regenerating cells or changing melanin production, make the product a drug under FDA rules. In Canada, "removes wrinkles" and "treats acne" are therapeutic claims, while "wrinkles look reduced" is cosmetic.
Can a skincare ad say "clinically proven" or "87% saw results"?
Only if you hold the evidence for that exact claim. The UK ASA's guidance on creams says a line like "80% of women said it reduced the appearance of their wrinkles" needs a consumer use or perception test behind it, and physiological or cumulative effects need a much higher level of evidence. We put a number in an ad only when it comes from your study, worded the way the study supports.
Can skincare ads use AI images?
Yes for the set, the props, the light and the packaging, as long as the skin in the image doesn't show a result the product can't give. The ASA treats before-and-after images as objective claims and expects the after to be representative of what customers can generally get. AI image models smooth skin by default, so a skincare image needs checking for pores, texture and fine lines before it runs.
Who can skincare ads target on Meta?
Meta's Health and Wellness policy says ads promoting cosmetic products must be targeted to people 18 or older. The same policy bars statements of inferiority that attack someone's appearance or specific body parts, skin whitening or bleaching products that cause permanent color change, and claims to cure psoriasis (Meta Transparency Center, checked September 2026). A teen acne brand has to reach under-18s through organic content, not Meta ads.
