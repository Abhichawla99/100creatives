The definition

What does a performance creative agency do?

A performance creative agency makes ads whose job is measured inside the ad account: clicks, purchases, cost per result. The work runs as a loop. The agency writes concepts, each one a different reason to buy. It makes several versions of each concept, with different hooks, headlines, crops and sizes. The ads go live, the platform puts more budget behind the versions that get results, and the next batch builds on those and replaces the ones that faded.

A brand agency works on a different clock. It makes a campaign to be seen as a whole: a few hero pieces, made over weeks, meant to run for a season. A performance creative agency makes many smaller pieces on a schedule and lets the platform decide which ones earn the spend.

Agencies in this category differ most on who reads the results. Some only make ads and hand them to whoever runs your account. Some run the account too, and decide the next batch from the numbers. Some write strategy and scripts but leave the buying to you. Ask which kind you're talking to on the first call. The price, the contract and what you still need in-house all follow from the answer.

CPG specifics

What a CPG brand needs from a performance creative agency

A CPG brand sells a physical pack through stores as well as online. That adds three jobs to the brief that a brand selling only from its own site doesn't have.

Pack shots that match the shelf

The pack in the ad has to be the pack in the store: the current label, the current size, and a flavor name you can read at phone size. When the design team updates the front panel, every ad showing the old pack is out of date, and a shopper who looks for it in the aisle won't find it. Ask how the agency checks pack accuracy before delivery and what happens when a pack changes mid-campaign. A brand with many flavors and sizes also needs an image of each one, which is where making images without a shoot helps. A new flavor doesn't have to wait for a studio day.

Claims rules for food and supplements

Supplement ads carry the same claim rules as supplement labels. FDA doesn't pre-approve structure/function claims, which describe how an ingredient affects the body. The maker has to be able to substantiate the claim, notify FDA no later than 30 days after marketing a supplement with it, and say on the label that FDA hasn't evaluated the claim and that the product isn't intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease (FDA, Structure/Function Claims, checked November 2026). For food and drink, a safe working rule is that an ad repeats a nutrition or ingredient claim only when the pack carries it.

Whoever you hire, have your own regulatory or legal reviewer sign off on claims before an ad runs. An agency's copywriter is no substitute for that review. Supplement ad creatives covers the claim categories in more detail.

Retail-launch timing

When a chain listing lands, paid social can send people to the store with ads that name the retailer. Those ads have a fixed date: they need to be ready when the product is on shelf, not when the agency's next batch is due. Check whether the retailer allows its name and logo in your ads, and brief the "now at" versions early enough to get that approval. A short batch turnaround helps when a shelf date moves.

Before you sign

Five questions to ask on the first call

  1. Who buys the media, and who decides the next batch? If the agency only makes ads, someone on your side has to read the results and turn them into the next brief.
  2. What does one batch contain? Get the number of concepts, versions per concept, sizes, and the split between statics and video in writing. How many ad creatives you need sizes that count against your spend.
  3. How do you check the pack and the label? You want a named step before delivery, not a fix after launch.
  4. Who owns the files, and for how long can you run a creator's face? Billo's pricing page doesn't mention usage rights, so ask any creator platform directly before you buy a pack.
  5. What is fixed in the price, and what happens if you stop? A monthly fee, a per-video price and a quoted total behave differently when a launch slips.
