Amazon main image photography that passes the 1000 px test

The 1000 px test is Amazon's zoom threshold. Amazon accepts images from 500 to 10,000 pixels on the longest side, but prefers images larger than 1,000 pixels on each side so shoppers can zoom. A main image that passes also sits on pure white (RGB 255, 255, 255), has the product filling 85% or more of the frame, and shows nothing the buyer won't receive. Photograph the real product for it.

The zoom threshold

What is the 1000 x 1000 test?

It's shorthand for the image size at which zoom becomes available. Amazon's two public sources word it slightly differently. The Sell on Amazon guide says Amazon prefers images larger than 1,000 pixels on each side to allow zoom. The Dougal_Amazon post says a minimum of 1,000 pixels on the longest side for zoom, with 1,600 or more as the optimal size. A square image of at least 1,000 by 1,000 pixels meets both readings, which is likely where the "1000 x 1000" name comes from.

The product has to be big in the frame too. At 85% fill, a 1,000 px square gives the product about 850 pixels. Export at 1,600 px and the product gets about 1,360, which is more detail for a shopper to zoom into.

Upscaling doesn't add detail. A 700 px photo stretched to 1,600 px clears the number and zooms into blur and soft, stepped edges, and Amazon's guide asks for images that are clear, unpixellated and free of jagged edges. Shoot at your camera's full resolution and crop down, never up.

Size the file last. Crop to the product first, then export. A 6,000 px camera file cropped to the product and exported at 2,000 px clears the zoom size and stays well under the 10,000 px cap.

Real product

Can the main image be AI-generated or a 3D render?

Amazon's public statements don't settle it, so plan on a photo. An older post by Hazel_Amazon, an Amazon staff account, on main image backgrounds (it refers to a June 2021 deadline; checked November 2026) says main images "must be high quality photographs of the actual product," and that graphics, illustrations, mockups and placeholders aren't allowed. It also rules out text, logos, borders, color blocks and watermarks.

A newer thread asks directly whether photo-realistic 3D renders are allowed as the main image (checked November 2026). Roberto_Amazon replied that the requirement is a realistic, professional-quality image that accurately represents the product, that the policy doesn't say how the image is made, and that there's no official guarantee either way. His advice was to use an actual product photo for the main image to avoid suppression risk, and to put renders in the secondary slots.

One AI rule is explicit. A News_Amazon post (checked November 2026) tells sellers to tag product images that contain photorealistic AI-generated people with the keyword contains-synthetic-performer in the file's XMP dc:subject field, before adding them to listings and A+ content. It links a Seller Central help page on tagging that also returned only navigation when we fetched it. The rule matters most for lifestyle and A+ images with a person in them.

We sell AI imagery, and our advice is still to photograph the real product for the main image. It's the frame a shopper zooms into to check what's arriving, and a real photo of the physical product takes the question off the table. Before you upload

Why main images get rejected: a pre-upload checklist

There are no rejection statistics on this page. These are the failures the rules above describe, each with a check you can run in any image editor before you upload.

  1. Off-white background. Sample the four corners and the area beside the product's base with the eyedropper. Every sample should read 255, 255, 255. The light grey of a paper sweep or a lightbox often reads a few points short.
  2. Product too small in the frame. Draw a box around the product and compare its longest side with the frame's. Under 85%, crop tighter.
  3. Added text, badges or logos. Remove anything put on in editing: "bestseller" flags, feature callouts, borders, color blocks and watermarks. The label printed on your product is part of the product.
  4. Props and extras. If the scoop, the stand, the garnish or the second unit doesn't ship in the order, take it out of the frame.
  5. More than one view, or a cropped product. Show one product, whole, from one angle. Angles and close-ups go in the secondary slots.
  6. Still in the box. Shoot the product out of its packaging unless it's a consumable or a collectible.
  7. Too small or soft. View the export at 100%. If it's under 1,000 px, upscaled, or the cut-out edges look stepped, re-export from the original or reshoot.
  8. File type. Export a JPEG, which Amazon prefers.
  9. Out-of-date product. The Dougal_Amazon post tells sellers to keep images updated to reflect the product's current appearance. When the label, color or cap changes, the main image changes with it, on every variation.
  10. Untagged AI people. If any image on the listing or in A+ content shows a photorealistic AI-generated person, tag it before it goes up.

After the main image

Where AI imagery helps on an Amazon listing

Everything after the main image has looser rules. The Dougal_Amazon post says the additional images give you room to show the product in use or in context, highlight details and textures, show other angles or cross-sections, and add size comparisons or usage instructions. Those slots, plus A+ content and the ads that send shoppers to the listing, are where AI imagery can replace a shoot.

The accuracy rule stays. Every secondary image still has to show the product the buyer receives: the same label, color, cap and size. Start from a real photo of the product as the reference, and check each keeper at full size against the physical product. Expect rejects. When we built four campaigns by hand earlier in 2026 with AI image models, 361 renders produced 45 keepers, about 8 renders per usable image.
