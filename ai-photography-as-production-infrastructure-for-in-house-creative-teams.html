The definition

What does AI photography infrastructure mean for an in-house team?

Most in-house teams start with one person who is good at prompting. That person makes a strong set of product photos, and a few weeks later nobody else can make one that matches, because the light, the lens, the reference files and the failed attempts all lived in one head and one browser history.

Infrastructure is what replaces that head: a written, shared set of inputs and steps that produces the same look whoever runs it, plus a review step that stops the photos that shouldn't ship. Which tool you pick matters less than whether the rules, references and reviews are written down where the whole team can find them.

This page covers still images for product pages, ads and campaigns. Video needs its own setup.

The parts

The seven parts of an in-house AI photo pipeline

A pipeline that survives a new hire has these seven parts. Each one is a file or a decision with an owner, so you can check today which ones you have.

01Brand rules, written down

Colors as hex codes, the light (source, direction, softness), lens and framing, grade and grain, casting and styling, and a never list of props and looks the brand has ruled out. 100 Creatives covers the same ground in its brand intake: visual language, model preferences, colorways and export specs. How to brief an agency for AI creative lists what a brief needs, which makes a good first draft. A rule nobody wrote down gets reinterpreted by each person who prompts.

02Product references

Clean, current photos of every product from several angles, label readable, one folder per product. The model works from what it is given, so an old label in the folder becomes an old label in the ad. Retire the old pack on the day the new one ships.

03Prompt and workflow templates

One template per shot type (packshot, product in a setting, product in a hand, product on a model), marking what stays fixed and what a person fills in. On a node canvas the template is the canvas. In a self-serve tool it is the saved brand and its settings. Put the model name and a date on each one.

04A review and approval step

A named person checks each photo against the real product: label text, logo, closure, proportions, colorway, and any hands or faces. Then someone with authority approves it for use. How to review AI creative from an agency sets out a two-pass check that works as well on your own team's output.

05File specs and naming

The sizes and crops each channel needs, file format, color profile, and a naming pattern for product, shot type, size and version. Without it, the team remakes images it already has because nobody can find them.

06Who owns the tools and accounts

Decide whose card pays for the tool and the model fees, whose login holds the brand files, and what happens to both when that person leaves. A pipeline built in a freelancer's personal account leaves with the freelancer.

07Usage rights

Read each tool's terms on who owns the output and where an AI-made face can be reused. Copyright in AI-made images depends on the law where you are; the US Copyright Office published Part 2 of its AI report, on the copyrightability of AI outputs, on January 29, 2025 (U.S. Copyright Office, checked November 2026). Ask counsel before the photos run in paid media.

What goes wrong

Where in-house AI photo pipelines break

Label and shape drift

Image models redraw the product every time. Small label text comes back misspelled, a logo moves, a cap changes shape, a bottle gets taller. Clean references and a prompt that says to reproduce the product exactly help. A reviewer who reads the label at full size catches the rest.

A different look from each person

Two people prompting from memory describe the same light in different words and end up with two brands. Shared templates and written rules fix most of it. When someone finds a better prompt, it goes into the shared template the same day.

No review step

Under deadline, teams skip review on photos that look fine at a glance, and those are the ones that ship with a wrong label. Put a name next to the approval step, and keep the rejects with a line on why each failed so the next person doesn't repeat it.

The reject rate

Most renders get thrown away. Across our four hand-built 2026 campaigns, 361 renders produced 45 keepers, about 8 renders per usable photo. The average hides a wide spread:

Models change under you

The image models behind every tool get replaced, and a template tuned for one may need rewriting for the next. When that happens, remake three approved photos from your most-used templates and compare them before the team carries on.
