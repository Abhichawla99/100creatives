AG1-style supplement product photography for PDPs

"AG1-style" is shorthand for a look that premium greens-powder brands such as AG1 popularized: the pack on a saturated brand-color backdrop, one hard directional light, and close-ups of the powder, the scoop and the finished drink. A supplement product page needs about eight frames to carry that look, plus a white-background main image if you sell on Amazon, and the label in every frame has to match the pack the customer receives.

By Abhi Chawla, founder · Last updated: 2026-11-13

100 Creatives sells supplement product photography, and this page says where we fit. It also says where a photographer, a studio or a self-serve AI tool is the better buy.

The look

What does AG1-style mean?

When a brief says "AG1-style", it usually means a handful of visual choices that you can check frame by frame:

  • A brand-color backdrop instead of white. A flat, saturated color, ideally pulled from the pack, fills the frame. White is kept for the marketplace main image.
  • One directional light. A single hard source from one side, so the tub or pouch throws one clear shadow and reads as a solid object.
  • The pack is the subject. It sits large in the frame with the front label square to the camera and nothing competing with it.
  • A label you can read. Brand name, product name and flavor are legible at thumbnail size.
  • Close-ups of the product itself. The powder, capsule or gummy, shot tight enough to see the texture.
  • A ritual. The scoop, the shaker, the glass and the counter it lives on, shown as a daily habit and never as a result.

No brand owns any of this. A tub of magnesium shot on its own label color under one hard light fits the description. What makes a set look expensive is consistency: the same light direction, the same backdrop and the same label orientation in every frame, so the gallery reads as one shoot.

The style has a downside. If every powder in your category sits on a saturated backdrop under one hard light, yours will look like the rest. Take the backdrop color from your own pack, and make at least one frame, usually the texture or the finished drink, that only your product could fill.

The image set

What images does a supplement PDP need?

A working set is eight frames. Amazon requires at least one image per product and recommends at least six. It requires images between 500 and 10,000 pixels on the longest side and says it prefers images larger than 1,000 pixels on each side so shoppers can zoom (Sell on Amazon, product photo guide, checked November 2026). Eight frames covers that, and it answers the questions a shopper has before buying a powder or a gummy: what is it, what's in it, how much do I take, and what does it look like in the glass.

Amazon

Colored backdrop or white: the Amazon question

The brand-color hero and the Amazon main image are two different photos. Amazon's own photography guide says that in most cases product shots should be taken against a white background (RGB 255, 255, 255), and calls white the default setting with Amazon (Sell on Amazon, checked November 2026). The full main-image rules, including category exceptions, are in Seller Central's product image requirements. Read them for your category before you shoot.

Make the white frame from the same angle, lens and light as the color hero. When the two match, the Amazon listing and your own product page show the same object, and the color frames can lead on your own site without looking like a different product.

Claims

What supplement photos can't imply

A photo can make a health claim without a word of copy. The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance (checked November 2026) tells marketers to judge the net impression of the whole ad, "including the text, product name, and any charts, graphs, and other images." Its worked examples include before-and-after images that, read with joint-health language, suggest an arthritis benefit, and a website that implied scientific proof through medical images. Any health benefit the ad conveys needs competent and reliable scientific evidence behind it.

For a supplement product page, that turns into three rules for the image brief:

  • Leave out lab props you can't back up. Glassware, white coats and molecule graphics in an ingredient frame read as a science claim.
  • Skip before-and-after frames unless you can substantiate the result they show.
  • Put only claims already on your label into the image. A badge or headline on an image is a claim too. Our supplement ad creative guide covers what those can say, including the FDA's structure/function claim limits.

If the same frames will run as ads, Meta, TikTok and Google each add their own health rules on top. Our page on health and wellness ad creative sets them side by side.

Label accuracy

Keeping the label accurate when AI makes the images

The label in every image has to match the physical product a customer receives: the product name, flavor, net weight, serving count, the Supplement Facts panel and any seal printed on the pack. AI image models redraw small text, so they can garble it, invent words or change a number. A frame that looks right as a thumbnail can show the wrong serving count once a shopper zooms in.

Run this check on every keeper:

  1. Give the model a clean, straight-on photo or the print file of the real label as a reference, and tell it the label must not change.
  2. Open each image at full size next to the printed pack and compare line by line: name, flavor, net weight, servings and every number on the facts panel.
  3. Where the small text won't hold, put the real label artwork back in during retouching, or use a real photo for that frame.
  4. Look for seals or badges that aren't on your pack, such as "clinically proven" or "third-party tested", and remove them.
  5. Check the pack color against the physical product under neutral light. A saturated backdrop changes how the pack color reads.

Expect rejects. When 100 Creatives built gummy supplement ads by hand earlier in 2026, in Figma Weave with AI image models, 38 renders produced 12 usable images. Across the four campaigns we built that way, 361 renders produced 45 keepers, about 8 renders per usable photo. The supplement campaign came out at the easy end of that range. Plan time to review the rejects anyway.

Shoot it

When a real shoot is the better choice

Shoot it for real when the frame is evidence. If the page says the powder mixes clear in water, a generated glass of clear water proves nothing, so photograph the real mix. The same goes for any frame with a real person in it (a founder, an athlete or a customer), any result or testimonial, and a facts panel that has to be readable at full zoom when you don't have clean label artwork to work from. Our guide to when you still need a real photo shoot covers each case and the rules behind it.

One workable split is to shoot the demonstration and the label close-up once, then make the brand-color hero, the ritual frame and the ad variations from those references.
