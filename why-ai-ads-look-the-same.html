Why do AI ads all look the same, and how do you make yours look like you?

An image model fills every gap in a prompt with its most likely answer, so brands that write short, generic prompts get the same likely answer. Ads start to look like one brand when the prompt carries that brand's own decisions (exact hex colors, where the light falls, the lens, the grain) and a person throws away the renders that drift.

By Abhi Chawla, founder · Last updated: 2026-09-24

100 Creatives makes its images in Overs, an AI photo tool built by our founder, Abhi Chawla. Both are brands of 2587689 Alberta Ltd.

The short version

Write your look down as decisions a model can follow: hex codes, light direction and hardness, lens, grain and styling. Send each photo the right references, keep type and layout your own, and keep only the renders that look like you. In our four hand-built campaigns, that meant keeping 45 of 361 renders.

Why it happens

Where the sameness comes from

  • Gaps get default answers. Leave the light, the lens and the palette out of a prompt, and the model picks them for you. Every brand that leaves them out gets a similar pick.
  • Keywords instead of scenes. Google's own advice for prompting its image models is to describe the scene instead of listing keywords (Google for Developers, August 2025). "Premium, clean, modern" is the kind of prompt that advice warns against.
  • Borrowed boards. A mood board pulled from the same trend references, with its objects copied into the scene, gives several brands the same picture.
  • Too little thrown away. The first acceptable render gets used, defaults and all.
What we kept

What our kept prompts had in common

The prompts behind the photos we kept from four campaigns built by hand in 2026 ran about 140 to 200 words on Nano Banana models and about 300 on GPT Image. The Nano Banana ones followed one order:

  1. The subject and a pointer to the reference image.
  2. The background, named with a hex color.
  3. Light, described as something falling on a surface.
  4. The palette, as names plus hex codes.
  5. One sentence of camera and lens.
  6. A separate sentence about grain.

The decisions that make it look like one brand are specific. The color has a code, the light has an angle, the lens has a focal length and an aperture, and the grain is named.

One sentence

One sentence, added by hand

On our brownie job, all ten kept prompts asked for heavy film grain, and nine carried the same sentence: "Heavy visible film grain throughout." A person added it by hand, prompt by prompt. Only 14 of the job's 120 prompts carry it, so nine of the ten keepers share a sentence that 106 of the job's prompts left out.

Your visual DNA

Write your brand's visual DNA

Pull your best existing images and write down what they share: the palette as hex codes, where the light comes from and how hard it is, the lens, the grain, and how things are styled. That list is your visual DNA, and it goes into every prompt. Overs' guide to visual DNA has a template for writing it down.

Then give those rules the final say. In Overs, brand rules are applied last in the ten-step run, and the quality check only checks the work against them. It never rewrites the art direction.

Mood boards

Take the feeling from a mood board

If several brands copy the objects from the same trend images, they end up with the same scene. Take the light, the mood and the pace from a board and leave the objects behind. Overs reads a board of up to twelve images plus a note for its feeling and applies that to your product in your brand's style, and a dial decides whether your brand's own look or the board leads.

Type and layout

Keep type and layout yours

An image model drawing a headline picks the letterforms itself. Set type in a separate pass, in your own fonts and grid. Overs does this in its own typography step, setting a headline, subline and button on a finished photo and keeping the clean version. On the ad side, what makes a good static ad covers layout and hierarchy.

Selection

Throw away more than you keep

In our four hand-built campaigns, 361 renders produced 45 keepers: 3.2 renders per keeper on gummy supplement ads, 3.6 on trousers, 12.0 on brownie ads and 13.6 on a surf campaign carried across three scenes. Somebody compared each render with the brand and threw away 316 of them.

In Overs, those decisions stay on record. Approvals and rejections with reasons become brand memory, and a note that fixed an approved photo is remembered. Frequently asked
questions

Why do AI-generated ads look the same?

Because an image model fills every gap in a prompt with its most likely answer. Brands that leave the light, lens, palette and grain out of their prompts get similar defaults, and a first acceptable render gets used as is. Specific brand decisions in the prompt, and strict selection afterward, are what set one brand's AI images apart from another's.

How do I make AI images look like my brand?

Write your look down as decisions a model can follow: the palette and background as hex codes, the light's direction and hardness, the lens and the grain. Send each photo the right reference pictures, set type in a separate pass in your own fonts, and keep only the renders that match. In our own hand-built campaigns we kept 45 of 361 renders.

What makes an AI image prompt specific enough?

In our kept prompts on Nano Banana models, about 140 to 200 words in a fixed order: the subject and a pointer to the reference image, the background as a hex color, light described as falling on a surface, the palette as names plus hex codes, one sentence of camera and lens, and a separate sentence about grain.

Should I use a mood board with AI image tools?

Yes, for the feeling. Take the light, mood and pace from the board and leave the objects behind, or several brands working from the same trend references end up with the same scenes. What is a brand's visual DNA?

The measurable habits of a brand's best images: its palette as hex codes, where the light comes from and how hard it is, the lens, the grain and the styling. Written down, it goes into every prompt so the model has less to guess. In Overs, a visual DNA step records it from your website and existing photos.

How many AI renders should I expect to throw away?
