Why do AI ads all look the same, and how do you make yours look like you?

AI ads look the same because an image model fills every gap in a prompt with its most likely answer, and most brands leave the same gaps. Ads start to look like one brand when the prompt carries that brand's own decisions (exact hex colors, where the light falls, the lens, the grain) and a person throws away the renders that drift.

By Abhi Chawla, founder · Last updated: 2026-10-13

The short version

Write your look down as decisions a model can follow: hex codes, light direction and hardness, lens, grain and styling. Send each photo the right references, keep type and layout your own, and keep only the renders that look like you. In our four hand-built campaigns, that meant keeping 45 of 361 renders. To check an ad you already have, cover the logo and ask whether a competitor could run it unchanged.

Why it happens

Why do all AI ads look the same?

Every image model has a most likely answer for a scene it wasn't told much about. Ask for "a premium skincare bottle on a clean background" and the model has to choose the background color, the direction of the light, the lens, the depth of field and the finish by itself. It chooses what is typical, so every brand that writes that prompt gets a close cousin of the same picture. Google's own advice for prompting its image models is to describe the scene instead of listing keywords (Google for Developers, August 2025), and "premium, clean, modern" is the kind of prompt that advice warns against.

Three habits make it worse. Mood boards get pulled from the same trend references, and when the board's objects are copied into the scene, every brand that used that board gets the same set. Headlines get drawn by the image model, so the letterforms are the model's. And the first acceptable render ships, defaults and all, because nobody made a second or a tenth.

The volume makes the sameness easy to spot. Meta's engineering team reported that more than a million advertisers made over 15 million ads in one month with Meta's own generative AI tools, and built its Andromeda ad retrieval system expecting the number of ad creatives to keep growing (Meta Engineering, December 2024, checked October 2026). All of those ads started from the same set of tools.

What it costs

Does it matter if your AI ads look generic?

NIQ showed low- to high-quality AI-generated ads to more than 2,000 people and measured brain activity in about 150 of them. Viewers identified most of the AI-generated ads, rated them more annoying, boring and confusing than traditional ads, and even the high-quality ones produced weaker memory activation (NIQ, December 2024, checked October 2026). The study measured attitudes and memory. It did not measure click-through or cost per purchase, so treat it as a warning about recall: an ad people don't remember does less for the next ad they see from you.

Sameness also wastes testing budget. Five renders that differ only in background tint test one idea, and the results tell you little about which angle to make next. If you're planning volume, how many ad creatives you need and our ad creative testing framework cover how to spread a batch across real differences.

Five checks

How to tell if your AI ads look generic

Run these on the last batch you shipped. They take about ten minutes and need nothing more than an eyedropper tool.

  1. Cover the logo. Could the ad run for your closest competitor without anyone noticing? If it could, the brand lives only in the logo.
  2. Name the light. Say where the key light comes from and how hard it is. If you can't, the model chose it.
  3. Sample the colors. Pull the background and shadow colors with an eyedropper and compare them with your palette's hex codes.
  4. Look at the type. If the headline letterforms came out of the image model, they aren't your fonts.
  5. Line up 20 renders. Put the last 20 side by side. Repeated backgrounds, camera angles and depth of field mean the prompt is carrying defaults.

If an ad fails only the first check, the look is right and the scene is generic. Fix the scene before you touch the style.

What we kept

What our kept prompts had in common

The prompts behind the photos we kept from four campaigns built by hand in 2026 ran about 140 to 200 words on Nano Banana models and about 300 on GPT Image. The Nano Banana ones followed one order:

  1. The subject and a pointer to the reference image.
  2. The background, named with a hex color.
  3. Light, described as something falling on a surface.
  4. The palette, as names plus hex codes.
  5. One sentence of camera and lens.
  6. A separate sentence about grain.

The color has a code, the light has an angle, the lens has a focal length and an aperture, and the grain is named. Those four details are what make it look like one brand.

One sentence

One sentence, added by hand

On our brownie job, all ten kept prompts asked for heavy film grain, and nine carried the same sentence: "Heavy visible film grain throughout." A person added it by hand, prompt by prompt. Only 14 of the job's 120 prompts carry it, so nine of the ten keepers share a sentence that 106 of the job's prompts left out.

Your visual DNA

Write your brand's visual DNA

Pull your best existing images and write down what they share: the palette as hex codes, where the light comes from and how hard it is, the lens, the grain, and how things are styled. That list is your visual DNA, and it goes into every prompt. Overs' guide to visual DNA has a template for writing it down.

Then give those rules the final say. In Overs, brand rules are applied last in the ten-step run, and the quality check only checks the work against them. It never rewrites the art direction.

Mood boards

Take the feeling from a mood board

If several brands copy the objects from the same trend images, they end up with the same scene. Take the light, the mood and the pace from a board and leave the objects behind. Overs reads a board of up to twelve images plus a note for its feeling and applies that to your product in your brand's style, and a dial decides whether your brand's own look or the board leads.

Type and layout

Keep type and layout yours

An image model drawing a headline picks the letterforms itself. Set type in a separate pass, in your own fonts and grid. Overs does this in its own typography step, setting a headline, subline and button on a finished photo and keeping the clean version. On the ad side, what makes a good static ad covers layout and hierarchy, and static ads for Meta covers the placements and sizes the type has to survive.

Selection

Throw away more than you keep

In our four hand-built campaigns, 361 renders produced 45 keepers: 3.2 renders per keeper on gummy supplement ads, 3.6 on trousers, 12.0 on brownie ads and 13.6 on a surf campaign carried across three scenes. Somebody compared each render with the brand and threw away 316 of them.

The ratio tracks how much the image has to invent. A packaged product on a set background gives the model little to guess. A food shot with motion, or one model carried through three outdoor scenes, gives it a lot, and each guess is another chance to drift from the brand.

In Overs, those decisions stay on record. Approvals and rejections with reasons become brand memory, and a note that fixed an approved photo is remembered. Hiring it out

If an agency makes your AI ads, ask for three things

First, the written brand rules before the first render: hex codes, light, lens, grain and styling, in a document you can keep. Second, one kept prompt from your job, so you can see whether your brand's decisions are in it or whether it reads like "premium, clean, modern". Third, the rough number of renders thrown away per keeper. An agency that can't tell you is probably shipping first acceptable renders.
