What a supplement ad can claim

Three rulebooks decide what a supplement ad can say, and they overlap without matching.

The FDA sets the claim categories. Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA), a supplement can make structure/function claims: statements about how a nutrient or ingredient affects the normal structure or function of the body. The FDA's own examples are "calcium builds strong bones" and "fiber maintains bowel regularity." What a supplement can't claim is that it will diagnose, treat, cure or prevent a disease, because only a drug can legally say that. Labels carrying a structure/function claim need the disclaimer that the FDA hasn't evaluated it, and the maker must notify the FDA within 30 days of marketing the product with that claim (FDA, Structure/Function Claims, checked September 2026).

The FTC polices the ad itself. Its Health Products Compliance Guidance requires competent and reliable scientific evidence behind any health benefit an ad states, and customer testimonials don't count as that evidence (FTC.gov, checked September 2026). A claim that is fine on the label still needs the evidence when it moves into a headline.

Meta decides who sees it. Ads for dietary, health and weight products, including supplements, must target people 18 or older, and Meta bars claims to cure listed diseases, fat-pinching close-ups and timeframe promises without a qualifier (Meta Transparency Center, checked September 2026). The full breakdown of Meta's rules, and what the FTC adds on testimonials, is on our health and wellness ad creative page.

For a creative team, all three reduce to one habit: the claim gets written and checked before anyone designs around it. A headline redesign costs an afternoon. A disease claim that slipped through costs a rejected ad set, or a letter.

Six supplement ad angles that stay inside the rules

Most supplement ads that get rejected fail on wording, so the useful question for a brief is which angles give the copy room to sell without reaching for an outcome promise. These six do.

1. The ingredient spotlight

One ingredient, its source and its dose, shown large. Colostrum, creatine monohydrate, magnesium glycinate and ashwagandha all have a story that sits upstream of any health claim: where it comes from, how much is in a serving, how it's processed. The ad sells specificity, which is what a shopper comparing labels in another tab is checking.

2. The structure/function line

The headline is a structure/function claim the brand already carries on its label and can substantiate, set in a clean layout with the product. "Supports healthy digestion" can run. "Fixes your bloating" edges toward a symptom promise, and "heals your gut" toward a disease claim. The copy pass that turns the second into the first is quick, and it happens before layout.

3. The routine

Where the product sits in someone's morning: the scoop into coffee, the capsule next to the toothbrush, the sachet in a gym bag. Routine ads show use without implying a result, and they photograph well, which makes them a strong source for AI product imagery because nothing in the frame needs to be a real before-and-after.

4. The format comparison

Powder against capsules, one daily serving against six pills, a stick pack against a tub. Comparing your own format choices is safe ground. Comparing against a named competitor's product is a different ad, and it needs the same substantiation as any other claim.

5. The formulation story

Why the formula is built the way it is: third-party testing, what's left out, why a form of an ingredient was chosen over a cheaper one. Only run the parts that are true and documented. "Third-party tested" needs a named tester the brand can point to.

6. The offer

Subscribe-and-save, a bundle, a sample size. Offer ads carry the least claim risk of the six and usually run as the retargeting layer behind the other five. They can't invent urgency; a countdown to a sale that never ends is its own FTC problem.

Which of these works for a given product is a testing question, and what makes a good static ad covers the layout side of each one. How many variations to run at a given spend is on how many ad creatives you need.

Statics first, then video

Supplement brands usually have more candidate angles than budget to test them. Statics are the cheaper way to narrow the list: six angles, a few variations each, run as statics until one holds up. The winning angle then becomes AI video, with the approved claim carried straight into the script. The claim rules don't loosen for video, and a voiceover that says "cures" is the same problem as a headline that says it. Static vs video ads covers the tradeoff in more detail, and static ads for Meta covers sizes and safe zones.

Frequently asked questions

What can a supplement ad claim?
A supplement ad can make structure/function claims, such as "calcium builds strong bones" or "fiber maintains bowel regularity", which describe how an ingredient affects the body's normal structure or function. It can't claim to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent a disease, because under DSHEA only a drug can make that claim (FDA, checked September 2026). The FTC separately expects competent and reliable scientific evidence for any health benefit the ad states.
Do supplement ads on Meta need age targeting?
Yes. Meta's Health and Wellness ad standard requires ads for dietary, health, weight loss or weight gain products, including supplements, to target people 18 or older. Protein products and general food products are exempt from that rule. The same standard bars claims to cure listed diseases and promises of a specific result in a set timeframe without a qualifier (Meta Transparency Center, checked September 2026).
Is 100 Creatives a full-service supplement or CPG agency?
No. 100 Creatives makes static ads for Meta, TikTok and Google, AI video ads and the product imagery behind them. It does not make packaging, out-of-home, retail point-of-sale or distributor decks.
