Consolidating photography vendors across a multi-brand portfolio

Consolidating photography vendors makes sense when several of your brands buy the same kind of images, such as packshots, product page sets and ad variations, from different studios on different terms. Keep specialists for brands whose look depends on one photographer, for shoots in other regions, and for work that needs real people, locations or a product in use. Then move brands over one at a time, after a pilot on one.

By Abhi Chawla, founder · Last updated: 2026-11-13

100 Creatives makes product imagery without a shoot, so we would like some of the work this page describes. It also says where a photographer or studio is the better buy. Nothing here is legal advice.

The decision

When consolidating photography vendors makes sense, and when it doesn't

In a holding company or house of brands, photography vendors pile up one brand at a time: the photographer who shot one brand's launch, the studio another hired for a retail reset, whoever a third brand's agency books. Together they leave contracts nobody has compared, files spread across vendor drives, and no single number for what the group spends on images.

Consolidation is worth the effort when most of these are true:

  • Brands buy the same kinds of images. White-background packshots, product page sets, seasonal refreshes and ad variations in standard sizes look different from brand to brand, but the production steps are the same.
  • You can't answer basic questions across the group. What did we spend on photography last year? Which licenses expire this year? Where are the original files?
  • Every brand negotiates its own terms. Usage rights, file specs and revision rules differ by vendor, so a central team can't move images or budget between brands without rereading contracts.
  • Someone already reviews work across brands. A creative operations lead or procurement team has to own the vendor relationship, or consolidation only moves the mess.

Keep separate vendors where these apply:

  • The look is the photographer. A brand built on one photographer's eye can lose what makes it recognizable with a general vendor.
  • Regional needs. Brands that sell in other countries may need local talent, local locations and someone on the ground who knows the market's retailers.
  • Specialist shoots. Real people using the product, before-and-after results, food cooked and plated, fine jewelry macro and anything shot on location need a camera and a specialist. Our guide on when you still need a real photo shoot goes through those cases.
  • A brand that may be sold. Whatever you decide, keep its contracts and files cleanly separable. A buyer will ask what the brand owns.

For most portfolios the sensible end state is a short roster rather than one vendor. One main vendor handles repeatable product and campaign imagery, and a few specialists cover the rest, all on the same contract template.

The audit

How to audit the photography vendors you already have

Start with one sheet, one row per vendor per brand. A studio that works for three of your brands gets three rows, because the terms can differ for each. Fill it from contracts, statements of work and invoices. Where a contract says nothing on a column, write “silent”. Those gaps are what the new contract has to fix.

Ownership

Who owns the photos: usage rights, copyright and raw files

Read the ownership column closely. Paying for a shoot doesn't by itself make you the copyright owner.

In the US, copyright “vests initially in the author or authors of the work”, and a transfer of ownership is not valid unless it is in writing and signed by the owner (17 U.S.C. sections 201 and 204, checked November 2026). A commissioned work counts as a work made for hire only if it is made by an employee within the scope of the job, or falls into one of a short list of categories and both parties sign a written agreement saying so (17 U.S.C. section 101, checked November 2026). A freelance product shoot fits those categories only in some cases, which is why many photography contracts use a written assignment or a license instead.

Canada starts from the same place. The author of a work is the first owner of the copyright, and an assignment or license is valid only in writing signed by the owner (Copyright Act, section 13, checked November 2026).

AI-made images add a wrinkle. The US Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 that copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, and that with current tools, prompts alone do not give enough human control to make the user an author. Human work that is perceptible in the output, and creative selection, arrangement or modification of it, can still be protected (Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2, checked November 2026). So for AI imagery, lean on the contract. It should say what you may do with the images and that the vendor won't reuse your product references or brand rules for anyone else.

Raw files need their own clause. For a camera shoot that means raw captures, layered retouch files and outtakes. For AI work, the equivalent is the reference images, brand rules and prompts that produced the set. If you want any of them, write it into the contract with a delivery deadline. A lawyer should review the final wording.

The contract

Contract terms to standardize across every brand

Write one template for the group, with a short schedule per brand for what differs: the brand book, the approver, the channels. Move every vendor, specialists included, onto it as their contracts renew.

  1. Ownership and license. Assignment or license; channels, territories and term; exclusivity; what happens when the term ends. Pick a default the group can live with so nobody renegotiates per job.
  2. File delivery. Formats, color space, sizes per channel, a file name that includes brand and SKU, and delivery into your DAM rather than a vendor link that expires. Set sizes from your platforms' own rules. Shopify, for example, accepts product images under 20 MB and up to 5000 x 5000 px or 25 megapixels, and says 2048 x 2048 px usually displays best for square images (Shopify Help Center, checked November 2026).
  3. Source files. Which raw, layered or reference files you receive, and when.
  4. Revisions. Rounds included, what counts as a revision versus a new brief, and how fast revisions come back.
  5. Payment. Fixed quote or day rate, deposit, invoice timing, and who pays for a reshoot when the brief was met but the images still don't work.
  6. Brand separation and confidentiality. The vendor keeps each brand's references, unreleased products and brand rules apart, and doesn't reuse them across brands or for other clients.
  7. Exit. On termination the vendor hands over every file and document within a set number of days, in the agreed formats.
Brand identity

How to keep each brand distinct when one vendor serves several

Brands start to look alike when a shared vendor works from memory instead of each brand's rules, and when one person approves everything.

  • One written brand book per brand. Palette, light, backgrounds, props, casting, crops and a list of things the brand never does. If a brand has no written rules, write them before the move, from its best recent images.
  • A separate intake and reference folder per brand. References from one brand should never sit in another brand's folder.
  • An approver on each brand team. Central teams can check specs and budget. The brand team decides whether an image looks like their brand.
  • A side-by-side test every quarter. Lay recent images from two brands in one grid with logos covered. If you can't tell which is which, the vendor is applying a house style. Look for the same props, surfaces, models or lighting setups appearing across brands.

Both spec concepts above came from our team. They differ in light, setting and type because each started from a different brand's rules. Hold any shared vendor to that standard.

The pilot

Run a pilot on one brand before you move the rest

  1. Pick the right brand. Choose one with steady, repeatable image needs and a reviewer who has time, not the brand launching this quarter.
  2. Reuse a recent brief. Giving the new vendor a brief your current vendor already delivered gives you a baseline. Our guide on how to brief an agency for AI creative covers what to put in it.
  3. Measure the same things for both. Days from brief to approved files, revision rounds, files that went live, and cost per approved image from the written quote. Ask the brand team whether the images look like their brand. For notes and sign-off, see how to review AI creative from an agency.
  4. Test the paperwork. Run the pilot on your new contract template, and check that files import into your DAM with the agreed names and specs.
  5. Decide brand by brand. Move the next brand only after the pilot passes. Let existing contracts run to their end date where breaking them would disrupt a launch.

If the pilot uses AI imagery, budget reviewer time for the shortlist. When we built four campaigns by hand in 2026 with AI image models, before Overs existed, 361 renders produced 45 keepers, about 8 renders per usable image. The vendor should do that culling, but your reviewer still sees and judges the shortlist.

Frequently asked
questions

Should a multi-brand company use one photography vendor for every brand?

Usually not one for everything. Move the repeatable work, such as packshots, product page sets and ad variations, to one main vendor so terms, file specs and spend sit in one place. Keep specialists for brands whose look depends on a particular photographer, for shoots in other regions, and for anything that needs real people, locations or a product demonstrated in use.

How do I audit photography vendors across our brands?

Build one sheet with a row per vendor per brand. Record spend from your invoices, what they make, turnaround from brief to final files, usage rights as written in the contract, who owns copyright and raw files, file specs, revision terms, payment terms and when the contract ends. Fill it from contracts and invoices, not memory. Where a contract says nothing, write that down.

Who owns product photos we paid a photographer to take?

Under US and Canadian copyright law, the photographer owns them by default unless the photographer is your employee or a signed written agreement transfers them. Paying for the shoot alone doesn't transfer copyright. Without one, you have only the license the contract grants, such as set channels, territory and term. Check each contract, and ask a lawyer before you rely on any of this.

Can one photography vendor keep several brands looking different?

Yes, if each brand has its own written brand book, its own reference folder and its own approver. Test it by laying recent images from two brands in one grid with logos covered. If you can't tell which brand is which, the vendor is applying a house style, and you should fix the brand books or keep that brand with a separate vendor.

How do I run a pilot before consolidating photography vendors?

Pick one brand with steady, repeatable image needs and a reviewer who has time. Reuse a recent brief so you have a baseline from the current vendor. Track time to approved files, revision rounds, files that went live and cost per approved image. Check the files against your spec sheet and DAM import before you move a second brand.
