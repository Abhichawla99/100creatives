What a food or drink ad has to do in a feed

A food ad on Meta competes with a friend's dinner photo, and it loses if it looks like an ad for something you can't taste. People decide in the time it takes to scroll past, so the image carries the appetite and the first line carries the reason. "Salted caramel, 12g protein" beats "Indulge responsibly" because the viewer knows what's in the cup.

The cues that make food read as edible are physical ones. Yogurt needs a glossy ribbon, not a flat beige surface. A cold drink needs condensation and a lit edge on the glass. A chip needs a visible crunch in the break, a coffee needs crema or steam, a sparkling drink needs bubbles that rise instead of sitting still. Get those wrong and the product looks like a render, which is the one thing a food ad can't afford.

The pack has a second job. On a shelf it's read from a few feet away; in a feed it's a thumbnail the width of a thumb. A flavor name in 9-point type disappears. So either the pack is cropped large enough to read, or the headline says the flavor. Placement sizes and safe zones for each Meta slot are on static ads for Meta, and the layout rules that hold for any category are on what makes a good static ad.

The honesty line: show what's in the pack

Food styling has always used tricks, and the law has always drawn a line around them. In FTC v. Colgate-Palmolive (US Supreme Court, 1965, checked September 2026), the Court held that a mock-up passed off as proof of a product claim was deceptive, and it gave mashed potatoes standing in for ice cream as an example of a prop that isn't offered as proof. The potatoes in that example are scenery. The moment an ad says "look how creamy ours is" and shows something else, it's a claim.

The UK's Advertising Standards Authority says the same thing in plainer terms. Its guidance on misleading imagery (September 2025, checked September 2026) uses a ready-meal example: an ad showing stew with potatoes and rice is misleading if the customer only gets the stew, unless the ad makes clear the sides cost extra.

For a food brand, the working rules come out like this:

  • The portion in the bowl matches the portion in the pack, or the ad says it's a serving of two.
  • Toppings, sides and garnishes that aren't included read as a serving suggestion, and the ad doesn't imply otherwise.
  • Inclusions match the recipe. A yogurt labeled "fruit on the bottom" isn't shown with fruit stirred all the way through as if that's how it arrives.
  • Color matches the real product. Pushing a pale green juice to emerald is the food version of a beauty filter.
  • Claims on screen are claims on the label. If the pack says 12g protein, the ad can too; it shouldn't add a number the label doesn't carry.

AI image tools make this line easier to cross by accident. Image models like abundance: they add strawberries, thicken foam, heap the bowl and brighten the color, because that's what the food photos they learned from look like. None of it is a problem as scenery. All of it is a problem when it changes what the product appears to be. That's why our review step compares each finished food image against photos of the real pack and its contents before anything is delivered. How to review AI creative from an agency has the full checklist if you're reviewing another studio's work.

The UK rule that decides which food ads can run at all

Food and drink brands that sell in the UK have a restriction most other categories don't. Since 5 January 2026, paid online ads for identifiable "less healthy" food and drink are banned in the UK at all hours, and TV ads for them before 9pm (GOV.UK, products in scope, checked September 2026).

The rule applies to 13 product categories, including soft drinks with added sugar, savoury snacks, breakfast cereals, confectionery, ice cream, cakes, sweet biscuits and cereal bars, sweetened yogurt, pizza and potato products. A product in one of those categories is "less healthy" if it scores 4 or more on the government's nutrient profiling model for food, or 1 or more for drinks. It applies to businesses involved in making or selling food or drink with 250 or more employees, so many founder-led DTC brands fall outside it, while the retailer or distributor running ads for their products may not.

The part that changes creative is the identifiability test. An ad is in scope if people in the UK could reasonably identify it as an ad for a less healthy product. A brand ad that shows no specific product can be exempt, but the exemption falls away if the ad shows the product, uses a brand name identical to a product name, or uses realistic food imagery that looks like a specific product (CAP implementation guidance, checked September 2026). For a brand in scope, that means two separate ad sets: product ads for markets outside the UK, and brand-only ads built without a recognizable pack or dish for the UK. Brief them separately from the start, because a UK version made by cropping the pack out of a product ad usually still reads as that product.

Drinks: alcohol, non-alcoholic and the pour

Drinks carry platform rules on top of the food ones. Meta allows alcohol ads where local law permits, never to anyone under 18, with higher minimums in some countries: 21 in the United States and 19 in Canada. Ads for non-alcoholic beverages are allowed "as long as they do not depict alcohol or consumption" (Meta Transparency Center, Alcohol, checked September 2026). For a non-alcoholic wine or spirit, that rules out the most obvious concept, a person raising a glass, and pushes the ad toward the bottle, the table and the occasion. Our CPG creative agency page covers these rules by country.

The craft problems are specific to liquid. A pour has to look like it's moving, with a clean stream and no splash on the label. A can needs the ring pull and the label seam where the real can has them. Carbonation should read as rising bubbles, and ice should look like ice rather than glass cubes. A bottle label is a curved surface, so text on it has to bend with the bottle. These are the details image models most often get wrong, and the details a drinks buyer notices first.

Five food and drink formats that work as statics

These are the formats we start a food or drink brief with. Each gives one image one job, and each can be varied cheaply once it earns spend.

1. The close-up

The spoon lifting out of the yogurt, the chip snapping, the pour hitting the glass. Tight crop, one line of copy with the flavor. It's the format that carries appetite best, and the one where accuracy to the real texture matters most.

2. The flavor lineup

Every flavor or size in one frame. Lineups answer "is there one for me?" and work well as the second ad a person sees. They're also where mismatched product photos show up fastest, since packs shot on different days rarely share light and angle.

3. The occasion

Where the product gets eaten or drunk: the lunchbox, the cooler at the beach, the table before guests arrive. It's the safest format for alcohol-adjacent and non-alcoholic drinks, and for UK brand-only ads when the setting can carry the brand without a recognizable pack.

4. The recipe or pairing card

Three ingredients and a result: the granola on the yogurt, the tonic with the spirit, the sauce on the pasta. Label clearly what comes in your pack and what the viewer adds, which keeps the ad on the right side of the serving-suggestion line.

5. The new flavor drop

A seasonal flavor, a limited run, a new size. The launch date carries the urgency, so the ad doesn't need a countdown or an invented "last chance".

Which format wins for a given product is a testing question. How many ad creatives you need covers how many variations to run at a given spend.

Frequently asked questions

What should a food and beverage ad show?
The product as the customer will receive it, large enough to read on a phone, with one reason to stop scrolling in the first line. Texture, steam, condensation and a clean pour do most of the appetite work. Garnishes and props are fine as long as the ad doesn't suggest they come in the pack, or that the portion is bigger than it is.
Can food ads use AI-generated images?
Yes, for settings, props and styling, as long as the product itself matches the real pack and what's inside it. The risk is that image models add things: extra fruit, thicker foam, a fuller bowl. The UK ASA treats an image that shows more than the customer gets as misleading, so every AI food image should be checked against the real product before it runs.
Are there restrictions on food ads in the UK?
Yes. Since 5 January 2026, paid online ads for identifiable less healthy food and drink are banned in the UK at all hours, and TV ads for them before 9pm. The rule covers 13 product categories, from sugary soft drinks to crisps and ice cream, and applies to food and drink businesses with 250 or more employees. Brand ads that show no specific product can be exempt (GOV.UK, checked September 2026).
Can drink brands advertise alcohol or non-alcoholic drinks on Meta?
Alcohol ads are allowed where local law permits, never to anyone under 18, and with higher minimum ages in some countries: 21 in the United States and 19 in Canada. Non-alcoholic drinks can be advertised as long as the ad doesn't depict alcohol or consumption (Meta Transparency Center, checked September 2026). A non-alcoholic wine ad can show the bottle on a set table, but not someone drinking wine.
