What a beauty ad has to prove in two seconds

Someone scrolling past a lipstick ad is asking one question: is that color going to look like that on me? Everything else in the ad is secondary to answering it. That's why the formats that work in beauty lean on evidence the viewer can check with their own eyes: a swatch on skin, a shade lineup across several skin tones, a close-up of the texture, the bottle next to something that shows its size.

The feed makes this harder than a counter does. A shade that reads as a warm rose on a Sephora tester can read as brown on a phone with the brightness turned down. A 10ml travel size looks the same as a 50ml bottle when nothing in the frame gives it scale. And the shade name printed on the pack is unreadable at thumbnail size, so the headline or a label in the image has to carry it.

The layout rules that hold for any category, such as one message per ad and a product large enough to recognize, are on what makes a good static ad. Sizes and safe zones for each Meta placement are on static ads for Meta. What's specific to beauty is that the image itself is usually the claim.

When the image is the claim

Beauty buyers expect some polish. The UK's Advertising Standards Authority says as much in its guidance on production techniques in cosmetics ads (updated August 2025, checked September 2026): consumers expect "a degree of glamour", and light retouching is acceptable. The line is retouching that exaggerates what the product does. Smoothing wrinkles in an eye cream ad, adding volume in a volumizing shampoo ad or lengthening lashes in a mascara ad all cross it, because the edit sits exactly where the product is supposed to work.

Filters get the same treatment. In February 2021 the ASA ruled against two tanning ads posted by influencers for Skinny Tan and Tanologist, because a filter had changed the skin tone the product was meant to deliver. The ASA's position is that filters aren't a problem in themselves; a filter that alters the result being sold is. The ASA puts the responsibility on the advertiser.

For a beauty brand running paid social, the working rules come out like this:

  • The shade in the image matches the shade in the pack under neutral light. If a lifestyle scene warms everything up, the swatch in the same ad stays true.
  • Skin in a foundation, concealer or skincare ad keeps its texture. Pores are fine; a poreless face sells a result the product can't give.
  • Lashes, brows and hair show what the product does on its own. If lash inserts were used, they fill gaps, not add length the mascara can't reach.
  • Before and after pictures get the same lighting and the same retouching, and the after is a result a customer can get. The ASA accepts before-and-after images on those terms.
  • Words in the ad stay cosmetic. More on that below.

AI image tools make these mistakes by default. Image models have learned from millions of retouched beauty photos, so they smooth skin, even out tone, thicken lashes and shift lipstick toward whatever color the model thinks is attractive. We explain why that sameness happens in why AI ads look the same. In a food ad that's scenery. In a beauty ad it's a claim about the product. That's why our review step compares every finished beauty image against photos and swatches of the real product before anything is delivered, and why how to review AI creative from an agency puts shade accuracy near the top of its checklist.

Cosmetic words and drug words

In the United States, what an ad says can change what the product legally is. Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, a cosmetic is meant for "cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, or altering the appearance". A product intended to treat or prevent disease, or to affect the structure or function of the body, is a drug. The FDA's guidance on cosmetics and drugs (checked September 2026) says intended use is shown by claims in advertising and labeling, including online, and lists claims that make a product a drug: restoring hair growth, reducing cellulite, regenerating cells, treating dandruff, treating acne, changing melanin production.

So "makes hair look fuller" is cosmetic copy, and "regrows hair" is drug copy. "Evens the look of skin tone" is cosmetic; "reduces melanin" is not. A copywriter pushing for a stronger hook tends to drift from the first kind to the second, and so do AI copy tools. Our ads use the claims on your label and in your approved claims list, and we don't add new ones. Skincare carries the most risk here, so skincare ad creatives covers it in more depth, and health and wellness ad creatives covers supplement-style claims.

What Meta allows for beauty ads

Meta's Health and Wellness ad policy (Meta Transparency Center, checked September 2026) says ads promoting cosmetic products, procedures or surgeries must be targeted to people at least 18 years old. The same policy bars ads for cosmetic products that contain statements attacking a person's appearance, body parts or hygiene, and bars ads for skin whitening or bleaching products that cause permanent color change.

That has two practical effects for a beauty brand. First, a brand whose buyers include teenagers can't reach them with Meta ads, so that audience belongs to organic content and creators. Second, the classic problem-solution hook ("Tired of dull, patchy skin?") is exactly the negative self-image framing the policy targets. The same product sells as well with the result as the hook: "Glow that lasts through a 12-hour shift", if the product can back it.

Six beauty formats that work as statics

These are the formats we start a beauty brief with. Each one gives the image a single job, and each is cheap to vary once it earns spend.

1. The swatch strip

Every shade in the range swatched on skin, with the shade names in type large enough to read on a phone. It answers "is there one for me?" faster than any headline. It's also the format where color drift shows most, so it's the first one we check against the real product.

2. The shade lineup on several skin tones

The same product on three to five models across the range of skin tones you sell to. For foundation and concealer, this is often the ad that decides whether someone clicks. The shades have to match the ones you sell, and the models' skin has to keep its own texture and undertone.

3. The texture close-up

The cream on a fingertip, the gloss on a lip, the powder breaking under a brush. Tight crop, one line of copy. Texture is the thing a customer can't feel through a screen, so the image has to do it.

4. The before and after

Allowed, with the rules above: same light, same retouching, a realistic result. It works best for products with a visible, immediate effect, such as a brow gel, a volumizing mascara, or a heat protectant tested against an untreated strand.

5. The routine

Three products in the order they're used, with one line each. It sells the set and answers the "what goes with this?" question that stalls a basket.

6. The fragrance mood

Fragrance can't show its product working, so the ad sells the bottle and the moment the scent fits: a hotel room at night, a linen shirt drying in the sun. Keep the notes in the copy and the bottle big enough to recognize on a shelf later.

Which of these wins for your range is a testing question. How many ad creatives you need covers how many variations to run at a given budget, and static vs video ads covers when a texture or application needs motion instead.

Frequently asked questions

What should a beauty ad show?
The product and the result it gives, at the size a phone shows them. For makeup that means a swatch or a face where the shade is readable, for hair it means the texture after use, and for fragrance it means the bottle plus a mood the scent fits. Keep one idea per ad, and make sure the shade in the image matches the shade in the pack.
Can beauty ads use AI images or filters?
Yes for sets, props, lighting and models, as long as nothing in the image exaggerates what the product does. The UK ASA ruled in 2021 that filters which change the effect being sold, such as skin tone in a tanning ad, make the ad misleading. The same test applies to AI retouching: smoothing skin in a foundation ad or lengthening lashes in a mascara ad crosses the line.
Can a makeup ad use before-and-after images?
Yes, if the before and after were treated the same way in post-production and the after shows a result a customer can get. The ASA's cosmetics guidance (updated August 2025) accepts before-and-after images on those terms and treats retouching aimed at the area the product works on, such as lashes for a mascara, as misleading. Small print doesn't rescue an exaggerated image.
Who can beauty ads target on Meta?
Meta's Health and Wellness policy says ads promoting cosmetic products, procedures or surgeries must be targeted to people 18 or older, and the ads can't contain negative statements that attack a person's appearance or body parts (Meta Transparency Center, checked September 2026). A beauty brand with a teen audience has to reach it through organic content or other channels, not Meta ads.
