Collab and capsule campaign imagery: settle approvals and ownership before the first image

By Abhi Chawla, founder · Last updated: 2026-10-08

Collab and capsule campaign imagery for an apparel brand needs one thing a normal drop doesn't: a written agreement between the two brands on who approves each image, how long review takes, where and for how long each side may use the photos, and what happens to them after the capsule sells out. Settle that first, photograph the capsule on real people once, and build the launch ads, both brands' placement sizes and the post-drop variants from those approved photos.

What a collab campaign needs that a normal drop doesn't

A normal drop has one owner. One creative director signs off, one brand book applies, and the images live on your site and your ad account for as long as you like. A collab or capsule with a partner brand changes three of those at once.

Two approvers. Your partner will want to see every image that carries its name, its logo or its designs. That review is often slower than yours, and if nobody sets the window in writing, it lands in the week before launch.

Two sets of rules. Each brand has its own casting preferences, color rules, logo spacing and words it never uses. A frame that passes your brand book can fail theirs on a detail neither team thought to mention, such as which logo sits on the left in a lockup.

A limited life. The capsule sells for weeks, but the images keep circulating. Your ad account, your partner's account, retailer pages and press coverage all hold copies. If the agreement says nothing about use after the term, you find out what each side assumed when one of you runs an old image a year later.

A collab with a celebrity, artist or athlete adds a fourth: their likeness and approval rights, which usually sit in their own contract and run on their own schedule.

Five things to put in the collab agreement before any image is made

Morgan Lewis's September 2026 guide to brand partnership contracts lists the approval and licensing terms these deals need: who is authorized to approve materials, the time allowed for review, the number of review and revision cycles, and whether previously approved materials may be adapted to other formats without a new approval (Morgan Lewis, September 2026, checked October 2026). For a capsule campaign, that becomes five decisions.

1. One named approver per brand, and a review window in days. Trademark licenses have long required the mark owner to pre-approve all advertising that uses the mark, because an owner that doesn't control use of its mark can lose its rights in it (Knobbe Martens in the New York Law Journal, checked October 2026). The same article notes that a ten-day approval window breaks down for social posting. Agree a shorter window for ads, in writing, and name the person, not the department.

2. Whether an approved image can be resized and recut. One launch image becomes a 4:5 feed ad, a 9:16 story, a 1:1 catalog tile and a retailer banner. If every crop counts as a new image for approval, your partner reviews the same photo five times. Ask for approval of the image once, with reformatting to listed sizes allowed.

3. Where each brand may use the images, and for how long. Morgan Lewis recommends licenses that set the scope, purpose, territory, media and duration. Write down the channels (your site, your ad accounts, email, retailer pages, press), the countries, and the end date.

4. What happens after the term. Decide whether both brands stop using the joint images, or whether either may keep a version with the partner's logo removed. The same guide says to settle this early, before the jointly created assets gain value.

5. Whether AI-generated or AI-edited images are allowed, and how they are labeled. Some brands ban generated images outright; others allow new settings but not generated faces. Get the answer before you brief anything, and agree who handles disclosure where the law requires it (covered below).

This page is not legal advice. Have a lawyer review the agreement, and treat the five points above as the questions to bring to that meeting.

Who owns AI-generated collab images?

Possibly nobody, in the copyright sense, which makes the contract matter more. The US Copyright Office concluded in its January 29, 2025 report that writing prompts is not enough to make a person the author of what an AI system outputs, and that purely AI-generated material is not protected, while human selection and arrangement of AI images, and expressive edits made to them, can be (Ballard Spahr summary of the report, checked October 2026).

In a collab that has a practical consequence. Each brand still owns its own logo, prints and garment designs, and those are what make the image valuable. But the generated background, light and pose may not belong to anyone. So "who owns the campaign images" is the wrong clause to rely on. The clause that protects you is the license: who may use these images, where, until when, and what each brand must remove afterwards. If your partner's lawyer asks for ownership of all campaign imagery, it is worth asking what that ownership covers when part of the image may not be copyrightable.

Running collab ads from both brands' accounts

On Meta, the format built for this is the partnership ad. Meta's developer documentation says partnership ads let you run ads with creators, brands and other businesses, the ad features both accounts in its header, and you choose which account is the primary identity. The ads deliver only with a permission in place between the two accounts; if you publish without one, Meta holds the ad in a pending delivery state until the partner approves (Meta for Developers, partnership ads, checked October 2026).

Three decisions follow, and they belong in the same agreement as the image terms. Whose ad account pays for which ads. Whose name leads in the header, which can differ by audience: your customers see you first, theirs see them first. And who sets up the permission between the accounts, because an ad stuck in pending delivery on drop morning is a common and avoidable failure. Set the permission up a week early with a test ad.

The creative changes too. A partnership ad already tells the viewer two brands are involved, so the image doesn't need both logos stamped in a corner. Spend the frame on the product and the reason the two brands belong together. Our guide to static ads for Meta covers sizes and safe zones for each placement.

Keeping a capsule consistent across every frame

A capsule is usually a handful of pieces sold for a short window, and the campaign has to make them look like one collection. That is where generated imagery is both most useful and easiest to get wrong. Useful, because one approved photo of a piece can become a dozen scenes in a day. Easy to get wrong, because small details shift between generations: a button count changes, a hem moves, a print repeats at a different scale, a model's face drifts between frames.

These two spec images show the check a collab approver will make. The dress, the model and the light have to read as one campaign across a close frame and a wide one, and the details have to match the physical sample: count the buttons, check where the front slit starts, compare the strap width. When frames differ, the sample decides, not the better-looking render. We are not presenting these as collab work; our portfolio has no collab spec piece, and most of our client work is under NDA.

Expect to discard most attempts. Before Overs existed, we built four campaigns by hand in 2026 with AI image models: 361 renders for 45 usable images, about eight per keeper. Keeping one face and body across a set was the hardest part; surf apparel on one consistent model took 163 renders for 12 keepers, and men's trousers on a model took 40 for 11. Disclosing AI imagery in a collab

If a generated image shows a realistic person who does not exist, plan the label before the ad runs, and agree in the collab terms which brand applies it. New York requires a conspicuous disclosure when an ad knowingly uses a synthetic performer, from June 9, 2026 (Cooley, January 2026, checked October 2026). Article 50 of the EU AI Act requires disclosure of realistic AI depictions from August 2, 2026 (Davis+Gilbert, checked October 2026).

Collabs carry a likeness risk normal drops don't. If the partner is a designer, artist or athlete, a generated face that resembles them, or resembles someone from their world, will look like an endorsement nobody signed. Brief generated people as clearly fictional and never prompt for a resemblance to a real person. Our page on AI fashion models vs real models covers consent and likeness in more detail.

A capsule campaign plan, working back from the drop date

This is the order we work in. It is our plan, not an industry standard, and the time each step takes depends mostly on the slower brand's review.

Before any image. Collab terms signed, including the five points above. Both brands complete an intake: visual language, casting rules, colorways, logo lockups and export specs. Agree the drop date, then mark each approver's review window on the calendar, slowest first.

The shoot. Approved samples on real people, plus a plain-background photo of each piece and close-ups of every co-branded label and trim. If a named partner appears, book their time here and nowhere else.

The first ad set. A small set of concepts goes to both approvers at once, not one after the other. Each concept differs by setting, person, reason to buy or format, so the review settles direction early. Our guide on how to brief an agency for AI creative lists what to send.

Launch. Finished ads in every placement size, the partnership ad permission tested, and the ads submitted early enough to clear Meta's review before the drop.

After the drop. Restock and last-sizes ads for the pieces still in stock, inside the license term. On the end date, take down what the agreement says comes down, in both ad accounts.

Frequently asked questions

Who approves the campaign images in a brand collab?
Whoever the collab agreement names. Morgan Lewis advises that partnership contracts identify who is authorized to approve, how long review takes and how many revision rounds are allowed, and whether approved images can be reformatted without a second approval. Trademark licenses commonly require the mark owner to pre-approve all advertising that uses the mark. Name one approver per brand and write the review window into the launch calendar.
Who owns AI-generated images made for a collab capsule?
Copyright may not cover them at all. The US Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 that prompts alone don't make a person the author of an AI output, while human selection, arrangement and expressive edits can be protected. Each brand keeps its own logos and designs. So the collab agreement, not copyright, has to say who may use the images, where, for how long, and whether they come down after the term.
Can both brands run ads with the same collab images?
Yes, if the agreement licenses each brand to use them in the channels and territories it lists. On Meta, partnership ads show both accounts in the ad header, and one brand is set as the primary identity. The ad needs a permission in place between the two accounts; without one, Meta holds it in pending delivery until the partner approves. Decide before launch whose account pays and whose name leads.
Which collab images need a real photo shoot?
The frames that prove the product: each capsule piece on a real person for fit, close-ups of co-branded labels, trims and hardware, the lookbook images wholesale buyers and retailers review, and any frame with a named athlete, artist or creator from either side. Generated imagery fits the work around those: more settings, both brands' placement sizes, regional versions and the restock and last-sizes ads after the drop.
How far ahead of a capsule drop should campaign imagery be ready?
Work back from the drop date and add both brands' review time. A workable order is: collab terms signed, samples approved, one shoot on real people, first ad set to both approvers, launch ads finished before Meta's ad review and any partner permission request, then post-drop variants. Well-known brands may need longer written sign-off, so put the slowest approver's window in the calendar first.
