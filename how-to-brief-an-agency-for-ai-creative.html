How to brief an agency for AI ads and product photos

An AI creative brief needs everything a shoot brief needs, plus the inputs an image model works from: clean reference photos of every side of the product that will show, your colors as hex codes, how your light and lens look, who to cast, and each placement with its size. An image model can only copy what it can see and follow what it is told, so every gap in the brief becomes a guess in the render.

By Abhi Chawla, founder · Last updated: 2026-09-24

100 Creatives makes its images in Overs, an AI photo tool built by our founder, Abhi Chawla. Both are brands of 2587689 Alberta Ltd. The same brief works for either.

The short version

Send ten things: the goal and placements, product references, the brand look in numbers, a few of your best existing images, casting, mood, messages and claims, a never list, deliverables, and approvals with dates. The template below has each one with an example. Every missing detail becomes a decision the model makes for you.

References

Reference photos: what to send

An image model redraws the product in every render, and it can only redraw what it has seen. Send a clean photo of every side that will appear in a finished image, plus close-ups of anything small: the label flat and straight, stitching, hardware, texture. A phone works if the photos are sharp, evenly lit and true to color.

References also have an order. On our trousers job, the prompt named INPUT IMAGE 1 as the approved character sheet and INPUT IMAGE 2 as the flat lay of the trousers, and told the model to match a fabric close-up reference exactly whenever one was provided. Overs does this for every photo. The plan names the references each photo needs, in order, and skips a missing one instead of guessing with the wrong picture.

For the views to shoot and how to light them on a phone, see Overs' guide to photographing your product for AI.

The look

Describe your look in words a model follows

"Premium" and "clean" give an image model little to work with. Google's own advice for prompting its image models is to describe the scene instead of listing keywords (Google for Developers, August 2025). The prompts behind our kept photos did that. They named the background with a hex color, described light as something falling on a surface, listed the palette as names plus hex codes, and gave one sentence to camera and lens and a separate sentence to grain. Here is one:

The product shown in the reference image sits perfectly centered on a white surface (#FFFFFF) surrounded by concentric rings of cocoa dust radiating outward like a bullseye target, created by a burst of powder frozen in perfect circular formation. Rich cocoa tones (#3E2015) grade outward to lighter cocoa dust brown (#B07D46) at the outermost ring. Sharp overhead flash renders every granule of cocoa powder in crystalline detail while casting a tight shadow directly beneath the brownie. The crumb texture of the brownie's top surface echoes the dusty rings below. Framed directly overhead at 50mm, f/8, focus even across the entire flat plane, composed as a perfect circle radiating from center. Heavy visible film grain throughout. Color grading references medium format Hasselblad digital, clinical sharpness, neutral whites, controlled warmth in the brown spectrum, pronounced grain, high contrast at center, saturation naturalistic and grounded in earth tones.

A kept Nano Banana 2 prompt from our brownie job, about 140 words. Lightly edited: brand names removed.

You don't have to write prompts to brief well. A brief that carries the same facts (hex codes, light, lens, grain) leaves whoever writes the prompts less to guess.

Mood

Mood boards: send the feeling

Send the board with a note that says what to take from it (the light, the mood, the pace) and what to leave behind. That way a board of foggy mountains turns into quiet, cool light around your product instead of mountains behind it.

In Overs, a board holds up to twelve images plus a note. Overs reads the feeling of the board and applies it to your product in your brand's style instead of copying what is in the pictures, and a dial decides whether your brand's own look or the board leads.

Claims

Messages, claims and the never list

List the angles you want to test and a headline for each, and mark what legal has approved. In regulated categories, our pages on health and wellness ad creative and supplement ad creative cover the platform and FDA rules.

If a real person or a real customer's words should appear, say so in the brief. The FTC's Endorsement Guides say a person presented as a user must have used the product, and New York has required ads to disclose AI synthetic performers since June 9, 2026.

Then write the never list: props, colors, poses, other brands' marks and anything your brand forbids. An image model fills every gap with something, and the never list closes the gaps you already know about.

What should be in a creative brief for AI ads?

Ten things: the goal and placements, product reference photos, the brand look in numbers (hex codes, light, lens, grain), a few of your best existing images, casting, mood, messages and claims, a never list, deliverables per placement, and approvals with dates. Every gap in the brief becomes a guess in the render, so numbers help more than adjectives.

What reference photos does an agency need for AI product photography?

A clean photo of every side of the product that will appear in a finished image, plus close-ups of small details such as the label flat and straight, stitching, hardware and texture. A phone works if the photos are sharp, evenly lit and true to color. An image model redraws the product in every render and can only redraw what it has seen.

How do I describe my brand's look for AI images?

In numbers and physical terms. Give your palette and background as hex codes, say where the light comes from and how hard it is, name the lens, and say whether you want grain.
